How fragrance discounting actually works

The discounts on a fragrance site are real money off something. The whole question is off what — and once you can answer that, almost everything else about how these sites operate stops being mysterious.

The reference price

"75% off" requires a number to be 75% off of. On discount listings that number is a list price — a figure the brand suggests and almost no one charges. A discount measured against a price nobody pays cannot be false. It also cannot be useful.

The list price is doing a job, just not the job it appears to be doing. Its function is anchoring: it sets the first number you see, so every number after it reads as a concession. You are not told what the product usually sells for; you are told what it was once suggested it might sell for, by a party with no interest in that figure being low.

This is not fraud, and it is worth being precise about that. It is arithmetic performed against a reference nobody can check. The percentage is calculated correctly. The reference is simply unfalsifiable — there is no observation you could make that would prove it wrong, which is also the reason it tells you nothing.

When everything is on sale, nothing is

If nearly every listing on a site shows 60–80% off nearly all the time, the "sale" price is simply the price. A discount that never ends is not a discount; it is the shelf price with a badge on it. Nothing has been marked down from anything, because the higher number was never a state the product passed through.

Which leaves a plain question with no easy answer: is today's number good? The only way to know is to know what the same product, at the same seller, cost last month and the month before. That is exactly the information the discount badge is standing in front of. It offers you a percentage so you do not go looking for a history.

Why the prices are genuinely lower: the grey market

Much discount fragrance stock is diverted or parallel-imported: real product, made by the brand, sold outside the brand's authorized distribution channel. It reaches the discounter through a route the brand did not intend, and the price reflects a supply chain the brand is not pricing. The savings are real. This is the part that is genuinely, straightforwardly true.

What you trade for it is generally support rather than substance. Manufacturer warranty coverage typically applies to product bought through authorized sellers, so a grey-market bottle often falls outside it. Storage and handling in transit are, in general, not guaranteed to the standard an authorized channel commits to. And if something is wrong with what arrives, recourse usually runs through the discounter's own returns policy rather than the brand's.

None of that makes the purchase a bad one. Plenty of buyers make that trade deliberately and come out ahead. It only means the cheaper bottle and the authorized bottle are not the same offer, and the price gap is partly the reason why. Take the trade with open eyes and it is a trade; take it without knowing about it and it is a surprise waiting for the one time something goes wrong.

Testers and unboxed

A tester is the same product a retail bottle contains, supplied for counter use: plain or unbranded packaging, often no cap, usually no retail box. "Unboxed" means much the same thing — the bottle without its presentation. They are cheaper for an honest reason. The brand did not pay to package them for a shelf, and they were never meant to be sold as gifts.

A tester is not the same purchase as a sealed retail box, which is why any honest price comparison must track them as separate products. Line them up against each other and the tester will always look like a better deal, because it is a different thing being sold. A site that folds both into one price chart is not measuring a discount; it is measuring the difference between two products.

Batch codes

A batch code tells you when a bottle was made. It does not tell you whether the bottle is genuine — a counterfeiter can print a batch code too. It is stamped or printed information, and printed information is the easiest part of any product to copy.

That makes it a useful answer to one question and a useless answer to another. Age it can speak to: if you want to know whether a bottle has been sitting in a warehouse for six years, the code is the right place to look. Authenticity it cannot speak to at all. A code that decodes cleanly proves that someone printed a plausible code, and nothing beyond that.

Affiliate links, from your side of the table

An affiliate link carries a tag identifying who sent you. Follow it and the retailer usually drops a cookie in your browser; if you buy within some window afterwards, the referrer is credited and paid a commission out of the retailer's margin. The price you pay does not change. There is no affiliate surcharge, and declining to use the link does not get you a lower number.

The window is where the incentive lives. Attribution periods vary by program from a few days to several weeks, and the longer the window, the more a referrer earns from purchases they had little to do with. The conflict is straightforward and worth stating rather than dancing around: anyone paid per click-through has a reason to call more things deals than the evidence supports.

This site will use affiliate links, and will say so on any page that carries one. The full disclosure — who runs this, how it is paid, and what that buys the reader — is on the about page.

Percentages are the wrong unit

20% off a $30 bottle is $6. 15% off a $285 bottle is $43. If a "deal" would not survive being stated in dollars, it is not news.

Percentages flatter small savings and flatten large ones, which is precisely why marketing prefers them. Dollars do not have that property. Convert before you decide, every time, and a surprising share of the excitement evaporates on contact.

What to do instead

Pick the product you actually want and watch its price yourself for a few weeks before buying — a running list in your phone, checked twice a week, will beat any badge on any listing page. Or use a price history somebody else recorded, provided they will show you the record.

That is what this site will be. Prices recorded daily, and nothing called a deal unless that product's own recorded history says it is one. The standard we will hold ourselves to is written out on the methodology page, including what we will refuse to count.
